Rocketry Airbrakes

Aug 2024 to May 2025

Redesigned a high power rocket airbrake mechanism with a 30% smaller footprint, equation-driven cam actuation, and synchronized deployment for equivalent drag in a much narrower rocket body.

Problem

This project was a redesign of a previous airbrake design. The original system was effective, but it was too large to fit inside a newer, much narrower rocket diameter while maintaining an equivalent amount of braking force. The redesign needed to preserve performance, reduce the footprint by about 30%, and still actuate the fins symmetrically outside the rocket body during deployment.

The mechanism also needed to keep servo load predictable and low enough for a reliable control system during flight. That meant the deployment geometry had to do more than simply move the fins: it had to turn motor rotation into linear motion while normalizing the torque load across the full actuation range.

What I built

  • Central actuator plate. The heart of the mechanism was a single actuator plate with curved cam slots. A bearing rode inside each slot so the plate's rotation translated into controlled linear motion.
  • Four-fin synchronized actuation. The plate drove four airbrake fins in sync using MGN9-constrained linear motion, moving the fins smoothly and symmetrically outside the rocket body.
  • Reduced footprint redesign. The mechanism was redesigned from the previous version to reduce its footprint by 30% while preserving equivalent braking area and drag performance in a narrower rocket tube.
  • Equation-driven geometry. The actuator-plate cam curve was specifically designed as an equation-driven logarithmic spiral, allowing the kinematics to be tuned for both travel and force behavior instead of relying on a generic circular or arbitrary profile.

Calculations

One of the most interesting engineered parts of the mechanism was the actuator plate. The curve that guided the cam motion was not arbitrary; it was derived from a fitted logarithmic spiral equation. The goal was to normalize the motor torque so the load on the servo stayed nearly constant as the fin actuated farther out from the body.

In the kinematic model, the actuator plate rotates by angle θ, while the bearing follows a curved cam path defined by the logarithmic spiral equation:

r = a ebθ

This relationship was chosen because the torque requirement needed to scale more predictably with actuation angle than a simple linear link would allow. The geometry was tuned so the effective reaction force and moment arm varied in a way that kept the servo torque nearly constant over the deployment range. In other words, regardless of how far the airbrakes were extended, the motor would see a similar load instead of a large torque spike near the end of travel.

This is especially important for flight controls and deployment timing. A servo that sees a constant or near-constant load is easier to drive consistently, operates with more margin, and remains more robust during the dynamic conditions of rocket deployment. The actuator plate geometry was therefore a key design lever for both mechanism efficiency and control reliability.

Machining and manufacturing

The actuator plate and the fin-support features were manufactured with a strong emphasis on precision. The cam slots had to match the intended equation-driven curve closely enough that the bearings traveled smoothly and without binding, while still staying stiff enough to handle the fin loads and the aerodynamic forces generated during deployment. The overall system was designed to be both compact and manufacturable in a student team environment without sacrificing repeatability.

The mechanism was machined from aluminum plate and carefully aligned so the bearing tracks and linear rails remained square to one another. The MGN9 rails constrained the fins in a predictable linear path, helping the four fins move in synchronization rather than relying on loose linkage tolerances. This was a crucial part of making the design reliable in a real rocket, where not only the drag profile but also the structural integrity of the deployment system matters.

The manufacturing work also reflected a practical engineering tradeoff: the redesign had to be compact enough for a narrow chassis while still producing a braking area equivalent to the previous design. That meant every feature had to earn its volume, from the cam path and plate thickness to the support structure and rail layout.

Results

The redesigned airbrakes kept the same braking capability in a substantially smaller package, while improving the deployment behavior through smoother motion and more consistent servo loading. By combining a reduced-footprint geometry, a synchronized rail system, and a torque-normalized cam profile, the mechanism balanced compactness, manufacturability, and control reliability in a way that made it suitable for a modern high-power rocket configuration.

Tools
